# Warren Buffett: stories he reuses

One or two lines each, with the lesson he draws and source ids. Counts are distinct sources in the corpus that tell the story.

1. **See's Candies.** Bought in 1972 for $25 million when it earned about $2 million after tax on $8 million of tangible assets; it went on to produce well over $2 billion of pre-tax income that was shipped out to buy other businesses. Charlie talked him out of being too cheap on price. Lesson: pay a fair price for untapped pricing power on little capital, and move the cash it throws off to better uses. (S016, S022, S024, S056, S064, S080; 15 sources)

2. **The Berkshire textile mill.** He bought a dying textile company as a cheap "cigar butt", took control partly out of irritation over an eighth of a point on a tender offer, and kept it running about twenty years too long. Lesson: a cheap bad business is a trap, and exits always come late. (S006, S023, S034, S098; 23 sources mention the textile business)

3. **Dexter Shoe.** Paid over $400 million in Berkshire stock for a shoe maker that went to zero when production moved abroad; the shares he gave up would later be worth about $5 billion. Lesson: overpaying is bad, paying in your own stock makes it worse, and structural shifts beat good management. (S034, S073, S074; 7 sources)

4. **Mrs. B and Nebraska Furniture Mart.** Rose Blumkin turned $500 into what he calls the world's largest home-furnishings store; Berkshire bought it on a handshake. "We gave Mrs. B a check for $55 million and she gave us her word. That made for an even exchange." (S057) Lesson: a low-cost operator with integrity and a narrow circle beats bigger rivals. (S005, S014, S023, S057; 21 sources)

5. **GEICO.** In 1951 he visited GEICO executive Lorimer Davidson in Washington and learned why the low-cost direct model would last; he sold too early in 1952, then bought heavily in 1976 when it was near collapse, and later called its advertising Berkshire's best investment. Lesson: a durable cost advantage is rare; back it, and keep spending to widen it. (S003, S004, S005, S072, S083; 28 sources)

6. **Salomon, 1991.** As interim chairman after a Treasury bidding scandal, he told Congress and the staff that losing money is forgivable but losing reputation is not, disclosed everything, and kept the firm alive. Lesson: get it right, get it fast, get it out, get it over. (S026, S032, S047; 18 sources)

7. **The Walmart mistake.** He set out to buy 100 million Walmart shares, bought a little, and stopped when the price ticked up; he puts the cost at about $10 billion. Lesson: anchoring on a price is a mistake of omission; when something is clearly good, pay up. (S007, S076)

8. **USAir.** A preferred-stock investment in an airline whose cost structure needed constant fixing. Lesson: do not enter businesses that need hard problems solved to survive; airlines grow and still lose money. (S001, S004, S023; 7 sources)

9. **National Indemnity and Jack Ringwalt.** Bought in 1967 for about $8.5 million. Premium volume later swung from $366 million down to $55 million with no layoffs, and it kept earning underwriting profits because nobody's job depended on writing bad business. Lesson: build a culture that rewards discipline over volume. (S006, S008, S087; 12 sources)

10. **American Express, 1964.** After the salad-oil scandal hit the stock, he checked whether customers still used the card and paid more for it, then bought 5% of the company. Lesson: separate a temporary blow from damage to the franchise. (S005)

11. **The Washington Post.** The stock fell by half soon after he bought it while the business itself barely moved. Lesson: a volatile price on a stable business is an opportunity, not a risk. (S004, S026, S066; 8 sources)

12. **Blue Chip Stamps.** A trading-stamp business that shrank about 99% under his ownership while he harvested its cash. Lesson: some businesses are dying; wring out the cash and redeploy it instead of fighting the decline. (S001, S005)

13. **Nebraska farmland in the late 1970s.** Land that could earn $70 to $80 an acre sold for $2,000 with rates at 10%, until the banks and buyers were wiped out. Lesson: momentum can run a long time, but the math always wins. (S005)

14. **Kraft Heinz.** He says Berkshire paid too much and misjudged how much power retailers would gain over weaker brands. Lesson: even a great brand can be a bad investment at the wrong price, and retail shifts change who holds pricing power. (S043, S045)

15. **From Graham to Munger.** He started with Ben Graham's cheap-stock method; Charlie Munger's blueprint was to buy wonderful businesses at fair prices instead. Lesson: what works on small sums does not scale; quality compounds, cheapness does not. (S023, S087, S096; 20 sources mention Graham)

16. **The $3 million hire.** A manager asked to add $20,000 a year of payroll; his boss told him to evaluate it as a $3 million decision, counting raises, benefits, and the fact that people are rarely let go. Lesson: price a hire over its lifetime, not its first year. (S077)

17. **Waiting to buy a house.** After marrying in 1952 he told Susie a house would eat his investable capital; they waited until the down payment was about 10% of his net worth. Lesson: protect the capital that compounds before spending on comfort. (S004)

18. **Paul Andrews and TTI.** A founder weighed selling to a competitor (best price, but staff cut and operations moved), a private-equity buyer (exit mindset), or Berkshire (permanent home). He picked Berkshire. Lesson: for a founder, the buyer's intentions can matter more than the top bid. (S094)

19. **Tom Murphy.** The media operator who built a great company from a run-down Albany TV station and taught him "praise by name, criticize by category." Lesson: choose your heroes carefully and copy them. (S097, S019, S088)

20. **The penny on a Coke.** One extra penny across 1.8 billion daily servings is about $18 million a day. Lesson: pricing power at scale is the value of a brand. (S027)
